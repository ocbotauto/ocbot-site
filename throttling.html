Throttling: why a GPU drops its own clocks

The card seems alive: the miner runs, shares flow — but the clocks sag and the hashrate floats. This is not a breakdown and not a "bad driver": the card drops its own clocks to escape overheating or power overload. That is throttling, and it is a useful signal if you know how to read it.

The short version (for the no-time-to-read)

  • Throttling is not a failure, it is self-protection. The card lowers clocks to survive; the question is not "how to disable it" but "what it is protecting against".
  • Two main kinds: thermal and power. The first means "too hot", the second — "power budget exceeded". They are fixed in opposite ways.
  • The monitoring signature: core clock falls as temperature rises or as the card pins against its power limit, and hashrate floats along.
  • Memory throttling exists separately — GDDR6X has its own limit and its own temperature sensor.
  • A voluntary minus 10–15% of watts almost always beats the same minus 10–15% the card will bite off by itself at the worst moment.

Thermal throttling: the card is hot

Every chip has a temperature past which the controller starts dropping clocks to stay below it. The mechanics are honest: the hotter it gets, the lower the clocks, the less heat. The card finds equilibrium "on the edge" — and sits there, losing performance.

Memory is a separate story. On GDDR6X cards the memory chips run hotter than the die and have their own throttling: the core is still fine while memory already sheds its effective speed (details in the hot memory article). So "core at 65 °C, all good" does not cancel quiet memory throttling next door.

The causes of overheating are almost always mundane: dust in the heatsink (the maintenance schedule), dried-out thermal paste, a hot room in summer (on heat), a suffocating case or a box without exhaust.

Power throttling: not enough watts

The second kind has nothing to do with temperature: the card has a power limit and cannot exceed it. If the load and the overclock demand more than allowed, the card lowers clocks to fit the budget. In monitoring this shows as a flat pin against the power limit on a cool card.

An important nuance: pinning against the limit is not always bad. On many algorithms a card sitting steadily on its power limit runs more efficiently than the same card unlimited at maximum clocks. The question is whether you chose that limit deliberately — or it sits "factory default" and gets in the way.

Telling the kinds apart is easy: watch the temperature and power graphs together. Clocks falling with rising temperature — thermal. Clocks low on a cool card pinned at the watt limit — power.

What to do in each case

  • Thermal: cooling first — blow-out, intake/exhaust, thermal interfaces. Not enough — cut watts: less heat at the same fan speed.
  • Power: if the goal is hashrate and temperatures allow, raise the limit within reason; if the goal is efficiency, keep the pin but pick clocks for it deliberately.
  • Memory throttling: lower the memory overclock — it is the main source of GDDR6X heat; the lost memory hashrate often returns as stability.
  • A floating hashrate, "fine then sagged" with no visible heat — check for periodic throttling: that is exactly what a preset sitting just past the edge looks like.
