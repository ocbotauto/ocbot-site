GPU fan as a failure predictor: what noise and RPM tell you

When memory or the die dies, a card does it suddenly. When a fan dies, it warns you for weeks: in slowly creeping RPM, in new noise, in rising temperatures at the same overclock. The good news: this is the most "advance-notice" failure a farm gets — you can catch it without opening anything, from charts and by ear. Below: how a fan ages physically, which signals are real, which are false, and what to do once you have caught one.

The short version (for the no-time-to-read)

  • A fan is a consumable with a real rated lifetime. Bearing life is counted in thousands of hours; in a 24/7 mode fans wear out first — before memory and the die.
  • The earliest signal is RPM creeping up at the same temperature. Dust or a worn bearing makes the impeller move air worse, so the controller spins it faster to keep the same cooling. All of it shows in ordinary miner logs.
  • Noise is telemetry too. Humming, rattling, interrupted whine — sleeve-wear symptoms appear before the stop: a bearing does not die instantly.
  • A dead fan = whole-node overheating. The card gets hot, and the lost airflow heats the whole rig row — up to thermal throttling.
  • Catch the degradation — schedule the replacement (or the whole cooler's), do not wait until "the rig is silent". A fan is cheap; a planned swap is an hour with a screwdriver. An emergency one means overheating, fallen-off cards and a day of downtime.

How a bearing ages: simple physics

Most budget and mid-range card fans use sleeve bearings: a shaft spinning inside a bushing through a thin lubricant film. The aging mechanics are plain: the lubricant dries out from heat and constant rotation, the clearance grows, the shaft starts to wobble, friction rises — and with it noise, power draw and temperature.

Pricier cards and PSUs carry ball bearings or fluid-dynamic ones (FDB): they live longer and behave more predictably, but are not immortal — just on another timescale. The rated life (MTBF) of mass-market card fans is around 30–70 thousand hours; at 24/7 that is 3–8 years, and with chronic overheating considerably less. How exactly your fleet is doing — your temperature charts will tell.

Which signals are real

RPM creeping up at the same temperature — real

The logic is simple: the fan holds a temperature. If, at the same overclock and the same room temperature, RPM gradually climbs over weeks — cooling is degrading: the bearing, dust in the heatsink, or dried-out thermal interface. All three are fixable if caught in time.

Cyclic hum and rattle — real

A bearing announces death in advance: periodic clicking, grinding, whine during spool-up. If the sound changes when the impeller reaches its cruise speed, that is mechanics, not "just how it hums".

Temperatures climbing at the same overclock — real (but general)

Rising temperature at an unchanged power limit and overclock is the most general sign of cooling degradation: dust, bearing, thermal pads, or all at once. It does not point specifically at the fan, but it certainly deserves an inspection.

One-off RPM spikes — false alarm

Single bursts after a driver restart or during a cold start are normal. The bad sign is a weeks-long upward trend, not one loud night.

How to catch it: ear, eyes, charts

  1. Listen during planned visits

    Stand by the rig for a minute and listen: grinding, wobble-flicker, "machine-gun" sounds of a tired bearing are audible even through the farm's overall noise. Once a month is enough.

  2. Watch RPM trends, not absolute values

    There are no universal RPM norms: cards from the same batch differ. Track direction: if RPM at the same temperature keeps stepping up, cooling is degrading.

  3. Keep dust in check

    Dust is accomplice No. 1: it adds drag to the impeller and insulates the heatsinks. A quarterly blast of compressed air saves both bearings and radiators.

  4. Replace fans at the first real signals

    Planned replacement is a couple of inexpensive fans and an hour with a screwdriver. Emergency replacement is overheating, fallen cards, a day of downtime. The second costs an order of magnitude more.
