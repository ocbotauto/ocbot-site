Breaking In a New GPU: The First Two Weeks in Your Rig

A new card arrives and your hands itch to drop it into the rig with your battle-tested overclock. Hold on: the first days of operation reveal nearly all factory defects, and right now you hold the strongest warranty position. A burn-in takes two weeks and almost none of your time — but it can save months of arguing with the seller.

TL;DR (for those skipping the read)

  • Most hidden defects surface in the first days. A card that survives two weeks under load will most likely work for years.
  • Stock first, overclock later. Validate at factory clocks: a defect at stock is a clear warranty case; a defect "after overclocking" invites disputes.
  • Stress tests and mining are different loads. Run both: some defects only appear in synthetic tests, others only in real work.
  • Seals and box stay until burn-in ends. No repasting or teardown before the return window closes.
  • Record the baseline. Screenshots of temps and hashrate from day one are your insurance if the card degrades later.

Why two weeks: the bathtub curve

Electronics reliability follows the "bathtub curve": failure probability is high at the very start (factory defects, micro-cracks in solder joints, marginal memory chips), then drops sharply and stays low for years, then rises again with wear at end of life.

The practical takeaway: if a card carries a hidden defect, load will expose it within days — artifacts, freezes, memory errors, overheating. The point of burn-in is to force that failure now, while returns and warranty still work, rather than six months later inside a production rig.

This is why the rookie instinct of "babying the new card at first" is the worst strategy. The correct approach is the opposite: for the first two weeks the card works hard and under observation.

How to test: stress at stock clocks

  • Days 1–2: synthetic stress at factory clocks. Rendering loops and benchmarks quickly expose chip and memory instability. A driver crash or artifacts at stock means an immediate return — do not try to "cure" it with undervolting.
  • Days 3–14: real mining. Put the card in the rig at stock settings and watch core and memory temperatures, hashrate versus identical cards, and the absence of recurring faults. Compare with its neighbors — "15 degrees hotter than everyone else" is also a defect (poor heatsink contact).
  • Only after burn-in — overclock. Two weeks at stock, then clock tuning. That way you know any instability came from your settings, not from the factory.
  • Around-the-clock is fine. Mining is a continuous stress test; the card needs no scheduled "rest".

Test the memory specifically under load: new production batches sometimes ship chips with poor frequency headroom — clean at stock, error-prone in mining. If that surfaces during burn-in, it is a warranty case.

Warranty, seals and evidence

Until burn-in is over, keep the card in "returnable" condition: no broken seals, no repasting, no cooler removal, keep the box and accessories. All of that comes after the card has proven itself.

On day one, take a few screenshots: temperatures, clocks, hashrate, serial number. If the card starts degrading a month later (temps rising, stability falling), those shots are your only proof that "it was not like this originally".

One more thing: if you bought several cards at once, burn them in simultaneously but label each one and note which slot it sat in. A week later you will not remember which of the eight ran hot. It also helps if you later decide to relocate or rebuild the rig.
