Home mining farm noise: where it comes from and how to cut it

A farm in an apartment hits its limit not on revenue or wiring, but on decibels. The good news: rig noise is almost entirely fans, and fan speed is watts. So the most effective "muffler" is not a soundproofed case — it is a sane cut in power draw. Let's go through the noise sources and what actually works.

The short version (for the no-time-to-read)

  • Noise = fans = watts. Minus 100 W per card is more audible than any "quiet" cooler of the same model.
  • Above ~80% fan speed, noise climbs steeply. A card at 60% fan is half as loud as the same card at 90% — for a hashrate difference of single-digit percent.
  • Rattling and pulsing is not "loud" — it is a dying bearing. That is no longer about comfort, it is about an upcoming fan replacement.
  • A soundproofed box only helps with exhaust. A closed box without airflow turns into an oven.
  • The rack and the floor are sources too: resonance and vibration through the feet can be more audible than the fans themselves.

Where the noise comes from

There are four sources, and each takes a different fix:

  • Aerodynamic fan noise — the main one. It scales non-linearly with RPM: the difference between 60% and 90% speed is the difference between "background hum" and "vacuum cleaner".
  • Bearings. A new fan makes air noise; a tired one makes mechanical noise: rattle, chatter, pulsing. That is a symptom, not a "feature" (details in the fan article).
  • Resonance. The rack, shroud panels and the floor pick up vibration and act like a drum membrane. Rubber pads under the cards and the rack feet remove a surprising amount.
  • Electrical noise. Rarely, but audibly: coil whine on some cards depends on load and clocks. It changes with the overclock — sometimes for the better.

Watts are the master volume knob

A fan moves air to carry heat away. Heat is watts. Cut the card's consumption — the card needs less air — the fans spin down. The chain is direct, and the effect is visible: moving from maximum overclock to an economical one usually removes a quarter to a third of the draw, and the rig goes from "audible through the wall" to "audible in the room".

Meanwhile hashrate barely suffers: the last few percent of an overclock are the most expensive in both watts and decibels. The economics of that trade is the same as in the mining winter article: count first, then give up hash — not the other way around.

A practical marker: if the cards' fans sit above ~80% permanently, you are paying in noise and bearing lifespan for single-digit percent of hashrate. Usually a bad deal.

Boxes, cases and placement

  • A soundproofed box works — with exhaust. A foam labyrinth on the air outlet muffles high frequencies; without a duct fan the box becomes an oven in half an hour, and the fans scream louder than before the box.
  • Distance and walls are free. Moving the rig from the living room to a balcony or hallway beats any padding. Just watch the temperature and humidity.
  • Do not muffle the intake. A carpet under the rig and a curtain in front of it "quiet things down" by choking the cards. Noise should leave; air should arrive.
  • Dust makes the rig louder by the same chain: dust → hotter → higher RPM. A regular blow-out is about quiet too.
